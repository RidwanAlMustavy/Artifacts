# Designing for Accessibility First

There's a persistent myth in the industry: that accessibility and beautiful design are in tension. That making something accessible means making it plainer, blander, more utilitarian.

This is wrong.

## The Curb Cut Effect

When cities started adding curb cuts to sidewalks for wheelchair users, something unexpected happened: everyone started using them. Parents with strollers, delivery workers with carts, cyclists, elderly pedestrians.

Accessible design creates better design. Full stop.

## Color Contrast Is Not a Limit

The WCAG 4.5:1 contrast ratio requirement sounds like a constraint. In practice, it pushes you toward bolder, more confident color choices. Low-contrast designs are usually the ones that feel washed out and dated.

## Keyboard Navigation as Architecture

If your interface can't be used without a mouse, you've built an interface with a single point of failure. Keyboard accessibility forces you to think about focus flow, which forces you to think about information hierarchy. The result is always a clearer interface.

## Screen Reader as Critic

Running your design through a screen reader is the fastest way to find content that doesn't make sense in isolation. It asks the hardest question: does your interface have a *coherent narrative*, or just visual groupings?

Accessibility is how you find out if your design actually works.
